# Metrics guide

Contents: definitions and analogies · which ratios fit which business · common pitfalls

## Definitions and plain-language analogies

Adapt these to the company at hand; do not paste them verbatim.

**Market capitalisation** = share price × number of shares. What the stock market says the company's shares are worth in total.

**Enterprise value (EV)** = market cap + financial debt (include lease liabilities if EBITDA is measured after IFRS 16) + minority interests + preferred equity − cash. The price of the *whole business*, including what it owes. Analogy: buying a house for 300k that has a 100k mortgage means you effectively pay 400k for the house.

**EBITDA** = earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. A rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting choices, which makes companies with different debt levels and tax situations easier to compare. Weakness: it ignores the money a company must keep spending on equipment and buildings.

**EV/EBITDA** = EV ÷ EBITDA. Roughly how many years of operating profit it would take to "pay back" the whole business. Lower often means cheaper relative to profits, but a low number can also signal a shrinking or risky business. Typical ranges differ a lot by sector, so only compare within a sector.

**P/E (price-to-earnings)** = share price ÷ earnings per share (or market cap ÷ net income). Roughly how many years of current profit you pay for. High P/E often means the market expects growth; low P/E can mean cheap or can mean problems. Use trailing (last 12 months) and mention forward P/E only if a source gives it.

**P/B (price-to-book)** = market cap ÷ book value of equity. Price relative to the accounting value of the company's net assets. Most useful for banks, insurers and asset-heavy firms; less useful for companies whose value is in brands, software or people.

**EV/Sales** = EV ÷ revenue. Used when profits are negative or tiny. Ignores profitability completely, so always read it next to margins.

**Free cash flow (FCF) yield** = free cash flow ÷ market cap. Cash left after running and investing in the business, as a percentage of the price. Often more honest than earnings because cash is harder to manipulate.

**Dividend yield** = annual dividend per share ÷ share price. Cash paid to shareholders each year. A very high yield can warn that the market expects a cut.

**Net debt / EBITDA** = (debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA. Roughly how many years of operating profit are needed to repay the net debt. As a loose guide, above about 3 to 4 deserves attention for most industries, but it varies by sector.

**ROE (return on equity)** = net income ÷ shareholders' equity. How much profit the company generates on the owners' money. Main profitability measure for banks and insurers.

## Which ratios fit which business

| Business type | Use | Avoid or be careful |
|---|---|---|
| Normal industrial, consumer, services | EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/Sales, FCF yield | – |
| Banks, insurers | P/E, P/B, ROE, dividend yield | EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales (not meaningful) |
| REITs, real estate | P/FFO, price vs. NAV, dividend yield | Plain P/E (depreciation distorts) |
| Loss-making growth companies | EV/Sales, gross margin, cash burn | P/E, EV/EBITDA (negative) |
| Cyclicals (mining, chemicals, shipping, autos) | Multiples averaged over a full cycle, P/B | Single-year P/E at peak or trough |
| Utilities, infrastructure | EV/EBITDA, dividend yield, net debt/EBITDA | – |
| Holding companies, conglomerates | Price vs. sum of the parts or NAV | Simple multiples |

## Common pitfalls to flag

- **Lease accounting.** Under IFRS 16 (Europe) lease costs are removed from EBITDA; under US GAAP operating-lease costs stay in. When comparing European and US peers, mention that EBITDA is not like-for-like and that EV should include leases only when EBITDA is measured before lease costs.
- **One-offs.** Restructuring, impairments and disposal gains distort earnings. Prefer "adjusted" figures only when the adjustment is explained, and say which version you use.
- **Different fiscal year-ends** make peers harder to compare. Use trailing twelve months where possible.
- **Currency.** State the currency of every figure; convert only when needed and say so.
- **Share classes and ADRs.** Make sure the price and the share count belong to the same class.
- **Stale or conflicting data.** Date every figure; if sources disagree, show the range.
- **Cheap is not the same as good.** Low multiples often reflect low growth, high debt or a declining business ("value trap"). High multiples often reflect strong growth, but also higher risk if growth disappoints.
- **Averages hide cycles.** A multiple below its 5-year average is not automatically attractive; ask whether the business itself has changed.
